## Executive summary

- **The obligation.** EU AI Act Article 4 (in force 2 February 2025) requires providers and deployers to support the AI literacy of their staff and others operating AI on their behalf. It is *horizontal* — every AI system, not only high-risk — and *measurement-silent*: the Regulation defines neither what "sufficient" means nor how competence is to be assessed, evidenced, or compared.

- **Two 2026 shifts.** The **Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**, in force 27 July 2026) softens Article 4 to a best-efforts duty to *support the development of* AI literacy and defers the high-risk dates (Annex III → 2 December 2027; Annex I → 2 August 2028); the hard competence duty for high-risk oversight under **Article 26(2)** is untouched. In parallel, the AI-Act standards programme matures on the technical side (risk, quality, datasets, robustness) but measures no human competence.

- **What this changes.** The need does not disappear — it moves from *mandated result* to *demonstrable diligence*. A best-efforts duty is discharged by pointing to a recognised, comparable measurement method; Article 26(2) keeps a hard duty for oversight roles. A voluntary, comparable competence-measurement standard would give content to both.

- **What we did.** Surveyed **23 frameworks** across six categories, deliberately including the individual-competence traditions a governance-only survey misses (SFIA, ISO/IEC 17024 and 24773, workplace-based assessment, situational-judgment tests).

- **The finding.** None is a *competence-specific, Article-4-native, comparable* instrument for the deployer workforce: SFIA and EN 16234-1 (e-CF) are general ICT-professional models with no AI-Act mapping and build-side competences; ISO/IEC 17024/24773 supply conformity machinery but no AI-literacy content; governance and maturity models measure organisations; literacy frameworks are education-scoped.

- **The gap.** A European standardisation gap: outside the ten areas of Standardisation Request M/593, unaddressed by any current CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 work item, and the natural complement to the human-oversight work (prEN 18229-1) — which standardises how a *system* enables oversight, but not the *competence* of the people performing it.

## 1. Context: why competence measurement is now a standardisation question

Article 4 is unusual among AI Act provisions: **horizontal**, in force since **2 February 2025**, and **silent on method** — it states the *what* (a sufficient level of literacy, proportionate to context and role) but not the *how*. Two 2026 developments define the present moment.

**The Digital Omnibus on AI** *(procedure 2025/0359(COD); COM(2025)836, 19 November 2025)* — EP first-reading 16 June 2026, Council 29 June 2026, signed 8 July 2026, published as **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744** on 24 July 2026, in force **27 July 2026**. Two changes matter here:

- **Deferred high-risk dates** — standalone Annex III systems to **2 December 2027**, embedded Annex I systems to **2 August 2028**. *(This does not empty 2 August 2026: several obligations, including much of the Article 50 transparency regime, remain live then.)*
- **Article 4 softened, not deleted** — providers and deployers must still *support the development of* AI literacy, but the amended article expressly does not require them to guarantee any specific individual's level: an obligation of **means, not result**. The Commission and Member States take on a parallel duty to support and facilitate literacy.

**Why this sharpens the need rather than removing it** *(a policy judgment, not a legal necessity)*:

- A best-efforts duty is discharged by **showing method**, not guaranteeing outcomes — and "support the development of" and Member-State "facilitation" both presuppose a common yardstick for what is being developed.
- **Article 26(2)** keeps a hard competence duty for high-risk oversight, untouched.
- Together, *a best-efforts literacy duty plus an untouched Article 26(2) is exactly the legal shape a voluntary, comparable measurement standard serves* — shifting its value from proving compliance toward **evidencing diligence**.

**The AI-Act standards programme is maturing — but not on the human dimension.** Under Standardisation Request M/593, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 is producing the operational standards:

- The technical side is advanced — prEN 18286 (quality management) reached public enquiry in late 2025 as the first AI-Act harmonised-standard draft.
- Human oversight is addressed by prEN 18229-1 — but it standardises how a *system* is built so a human *can* oversee it, **not whether that human is competent to**, nor how such competence would be measured.

**The result — a genuine standardisation gap:** a horizontal (now best-efforts) obligation about human competence, an untouched hard duty for high-risk oversight, a standards programme that measures neither, and — as the statutory emphasis shifts to "support" and "facilitation" — a need for a voluntary instrument that gives that support common content.

## 2. Method and scope

**Inclusion criteria** — a framework was included if it met at least one:
- Referenced in EU AI-governance practice or policy (regulatory instruments, harmonised-standard drafts, widely-adopted maturity/governance models);
- An established *individual*-competence-assessment instrument or standard for the digital/professional workforce; or
- An AI-literacy framework in current use.

We deliberately widened the frame beyond "AI governance/maturity" models to include individual-competence and person-certification standards — a survey confined to organisational maturity models would trivially "discover" the field is organisational, a conclusion built into the sampling frame rather than found in the world.

**Exclusion** — we excluded:
- Purely sectoral technical standards with no competence dimension;
- The author's own methodology (assessed separately in the interest-declared paper, §8);
- Frameworks not sourceable to a primary reference (several education-technology academic models were considered but dropped for lack of verifiable citations).

**Counting rule — twenty-three frameworks and instruments:**
- **21 scored rows** in the §4 matrix — each listed individually, including the five consultancy models (scored separately, not bundled);
- **2 method precedents** recorded but not scored (workplace-based/OSCE assessment; situational-judgment tests);
- The matrix is the authority; the count is reconciled to it here so a reader need not reconstruct it.

**Evaluation criteria** — three primary criteria that define the gap, plus two secondary adoption characteristics.

*Primary (gap-defining):*
- **Competence-specific for the deployer workforce** — is measuring the AI competence of staff who *use and oversee* AI (not those who *build* it, nor the organisation as a whole) a central purpose?
- **Article-4-native** — purpose-built for, or explicitly mapped to, the AI Act's literacy obligation? *(Assessed against Article 4 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.)*
- **Comparable** — does it yield results comparable across organisations under a common, defined protocol (the property a standard exists to provide)?

*Secondary (adoption, not gap criteria):*
- **Level of assessment** — individual, organisational, system, or model.
- **Openness of adoption** — freely adoptable, free-to-view, or proprietary/paywalled. *(Recorded because it bears on SME access; not a measure of standardisation adequacy — European standards are, by design, distributed by national bodies.)*

**Scoring key** (uniform across rows): ● yes / central · ◐ partial / one dimension among many / conditional · ○ no / out of scope. Every cell judgment is stated so it can be checked against the cited source.

**Register.** A landscape analysis, not a competitive teardown. Several frameworks below are excellent at their intended purpose; the point is never that they are deficient, but that none was designed for the specific task Article 4 creates — and where one already covers part of that task well (SFIA on individual levelling; ISO/IEC 17024 on certification machinery), that is an overlap to **build on, not duplicate**.

## 3. The landscape, by category

### 3.1 The obligation and the standards programme
- **EU AI Act Article 4** *(Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended by the Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744)* — the obligation itself, not a measurement instrument. Prescribes no curriculum, depth, or assessment method; "sufficient" is undefined by design.
- **Article 26(2)** — the untouched, harder-edged neighbour: deployers of high-risk systems must assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority. It is an obligation *about competence* with no measurement method attached — reinforcing, not diluting, the gap.
- **CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 harmonised-standard drafts** — prEN 18286 (quality management), prEN 18228 (risk management), prEN 18284 (dataset quality/governance), prEN 18229-1 (logging, transparency and human oversight). These operationalise the *technical and system* requirements of the AI Act. **prEN 18229-1** is the closest to the human dimension — but it standardises system-level oversight *design*, not the *competence* of the overseer.

### 3.2 Individual-competence frameworks and person-certification standards *(the category a governance-only survey omits)*
- **SFIA (Skills Framework for the Information Age), v9 (Oct 2024)** — a genuine **individual, work-anchored** framework; maintained by the not-for-profit SFIA Foundation, free to view online.
  - Assesses a person's demonstrated skills at one of **seven levels of responsibility** (autonomy, influence, complexity, plus knowledge and business skills).
  - SFIA 9 *integrates* AI into its existing structure (e.g. the Artificial Intelligence and Data Ethics skill, updated Machine Learning).
  - **But:** a general ICT-skills framework — its AI content targets AI/ML *practitioners*, not the broad deployer workforce Article 4 addresses; **no EU AI Act / Article 4 mapping**; no AI-literacy scoring protocol.
  - The closest existing individual-competence *framework* — and the clearest illustration that the gap is *specificity*, not the idea of measuring individuals.
- **EN 16234-1:2019 — European e-Competence Framework (e-CF)** *(CEN/TC 428, "ICT Professionalism and Digital Competences")* — the European Standard for ICT professional competences; the institutional counterpart to SFIA.
  - Four dimensions: five competence areas; **41 competences**; **five proficiency levels (e-1 to e-5)** aligned to EQF levels 3–8; sample knowledge and skills. First sector-specific implementation of the EQF; supported by CEN/TR 16234-2 (User Guide), -3 (Methodology), -4 (Case Studies).
  - **But:** describes the competences of *ICT professionals*; proficiency levels attach to professional practice, not the general workforce; no EU AI Act or Article 4 mapping; defines no assessment protocol — a common *language* for competence, not a *method* for determining that a given person holds it.
- **EN 17748-1:2022 — Foundational Body of Knowledge for the ICT Profession (ICT BoK)** *(CEN/TC 428)* — the companion knowledge reference. A body of knowledge, not a measurement instrument.
- **CWA 16458-1:2018 — European ICT Professional Role Profiles** *(CEN Workshop Agreement)* — a common set of ICT role profiles expressed in e-CF terms. Role profiles are builder- and ICT-practitioner-scoped; none corresponds to the non-specialist deployer-workforce population Article 4 addresses.

**CEN/TC 428 has already looked at AI — and scoped itself away from Article 4.** Its white paper *Artificial Intelligence (AI) and ICT Professionalism: Foundation for trustworthy and robust AI* (document N 557, June 2023, superseding a 2019 positioning paper) argues that trustworthy AI depends on the competence of the ICT professionals who *build* it:

- It identifies the e-CF competences with "key influence on AI" — A.6 Application Design, A.9 Innovating, A.10 User Experience, B.1 Application Development, D.7 Data Science & Analytics, E.3 Risk Management, E.8 IS Security Management — **every one a build-side competence.**
- It states its own boundary: it *"explicitly focuses on the essential role played by ICT professionals engaged in AI systems design, development, and its entire life cycle,"* and refers to deployers exactly once — as *"other stakeholders deploying or using AI."*
- The terms "AI literacy," "Article 4" and "workforce" do not appear; it predates the adopted Regulation (it cites "the current draft" of the AI Act and its Annex 1 definition).

This is the sharpest single observation available to this analysis. **The European committee that owns competence standardisation has examined AI competence and drawn its scope around those who build AI. The population Article 4 addresses — staff who use and oversee AI systems — falls outside that frame, and outside every other frame surveyed here.**

- **ISO/IEC 17024:2012 — certification of persons** — the horizontal international standard governing *bodies that certify individuals' competence*, ensuring schemes operate consistently and comparably. It is the conformity-assessment backbone for person-certification across professions. It provides the *machinery* for certifying competence — but no AI-literacy *content* and no Article-4 scheme built on it.
- **ISO/IEC 24773 series (Parts 1:2019, 2:2024, 3:2021)** — a worked, domain-specific application of the 17024 model, defining certification schemes for software and systems engineering professionals and enabling cross-border portability. Demonstrates the ISO route from the general machinery to a domain scheme — a route that has **not** been travelled for AI literacy.
- *Method precedents recorded (not competing frameworks, but evidence the task is tractable):* **workplace-based assessment / OSCE-style** evaluation from professional certification, and **situational judgment tests (SJTs)**, both of which assess individuals performing realistic tasks and carry documented criterion validity (SJTs: McDaniel et al. 2007, mean validity r ≈ .26 for job performance). They show competence *can* be measured at the individual, workflow-anchored level — but neither is AI-specific or standardised for Article 4.

### 3.3 Governance & organisational-maturity models
- **ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI management system)** *(ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42)* — organisational management system; competence appears as **Clause 7.2**, one sub-clause; certification is organisational and binary, with no competence-measurement method.
- **ISO/IEC 23894:2023 (AI risk management)** and **ISO/IEC 22989:2022 (AI concepts & terminology)** *(SC 42)* — risk guidance and the terminology backbone respectively; neither is a competence instrument, though 22989 is the vocabulary any competence standard must align to.
- **NIST AI RMF 1.0** *(US NIST, Jan 2023)* — voluntary lifecycle risk framework; the "Govern" function touches workforce, but no systematic competence scoring; no EU-law anchor.
- **Gartner, Deloitte, PwC, EY, McKinsey** — consulting maturity/readiness models. Where competence appears it is one dimension among many (Deloitte's own findings report "people & skills" as a commonly weak pillar); all are proprietary and organisation-scoped; none is an individual competence instrument.
- **BCG (ASPIRE)** — an economy-level readiness index; macro-scale, not organisational or individual.
- **Microsoft Responsible AI Maturity Model** (open paper; 24 dimensions × 5 levels) and **MITRE AI Maturity Model** (open, with a free tool) — both measure *organisational* process/adoption maturity, not individual competence.

### 3.4 System- and model-level frameworks
- **Google DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework** — model-level critical-capability levels; measures *model* risk, not human competence.
- **OWASP AI Maturity Assessment (AIMA) v1.0 (11 Aug 2025)** — open-source; targets *system* trustworthiness and security, not professional competence.

### 3.5 AI-literacy frameworks (education-scoped)
- **OECD AI Capability Indicators (2025)** and the **OECD–European Commission AI Literacy Framework** ("Empowering Learners for the Age of AI," review draft 2025) — the indicators measure what AI systems can do; the literacy framework is education-scoped (learners) and jointly authored (OECD, EC, Code.org).
- **UNESCO AI Competency Frameworks (2024)** — two frameworks (students; teachers), education-scoped, not mapped to the AI Act or to professional deployment.

### 3.6 Frameworks and methods raised in committee review (September 2026)

*Added after the LVS/STK 59 review of September 2026, where committee members identified further references. Each maps onto a category already above and is recorded here for completeness; none is scored into the §4 matrix (which remains the authority at twenty-three), because none alters its pattern — each broadens the evidence base while reinforcing, rather than narrowing, the gap.*

- **DigComp 3.0 — European Digital Competence Framework for Citizens** *(European Commission, Joint Research Centre; 5th edition, November 2025)* — the EU reference taxonomy for digital competence: **five competence areas, 21 competences**, with **four proficiency levels** (Basic, Intermediate, Advanced, Highly advanced; the legacy 2.x editions used eight levels grouped in four bands — there is no seven-level version). DigComp 3.0 integrates AI, including generative AI, **transversally across all 21 competences** rather than as a standalone module.
  - **But:** it is a competence *model*, not a measurement instrument (assessment tools are built *against* it); it is **citizen- and education-scoped** rather than deployer-workforce; and it carries **no Article 4 mapping**. It belongs alongside the literacy frameworks of §3.5 — the most AI-aware of them, and still not a comparable AI-literacy measurement method. *(The companion **DigCompEdu**, for educators, uses six CEFR-style levels, A1–C2.)*
- **AIRI — AI Readiness Index / AI Readiness Framework (v3.1)** *(originated by AI Singapore; now maintained at airi.foundation under CC BY 4.0)* — a self-assessment of an **organisation's readiness to adopt AI**: five pillars (Leadership & Culture; Ethics & Governance; Business Value; Data Foundation; Infrastructure & Standards), 15 dimensions, scored 0–5 across **five readiness levels** (AI Unaware → Aware → Ready → Competent → Catalyst).
  - **But:** it measures **organisational adoption maturity, not individual competence** — an organisation can score well while its staff lack the literacy Article 4 addresses. It belongs with the governance and organisational-maturity models of §3.3.
- **ISO 30400 series — Human resource management** *(ISO/TC 260)* — a family of HR-management standards; the training-relevant members are **ISO 30422:2022 (Learning and development)**, **ISO 30401:2018 (Knowledge management systems)** and **ISO 30414:2018 (Human capital reporting)**, anchored by **ISO 30400:2022 (Vocabulary)**.
  - **But:** these standardise organisational HR *processes* — how an employer plans, delivers and reports learning — not a scale that measures an individual's AI competence. ISO 30422 instructs an organisation to plan and evaluate learning; it supplies no measurement construct. A competence-measurement standard is the "what is measured" layer such a process standard assumes but does not provide, and its results could in turn feed ISO 30414-style human-capital reporting. This is a category not otherwise represented above (organisational HR-process standards).
- **ISO 10015:2019 — Quality management — Guidelines for competence management and people development** *(ISO/TC 176/SC 3)* — the closest *competence-management* neighbour: an organisational framework to identify competence needs, plan and deliver development, and evaluate it.
  - **But:** it is generic (not AI-specific), organisation-facing, and — like ISO 30422 — a process framework, not a tiered individual-competence measurement instrument. Numerically "10-something," not 30-something, but it is routinely cited alongside the 30400 family and named here because the boundary it sets is the same one.
- **ISO/IEC 330xx process-assessment family** *(ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 7; successor to the withdrawn ISO/IEC 15504, "SPICE")* — recorded as a measurement-*methodology* precedent, in the same spirit as the OSCE/SJT precedents in §3.2, rather than as a competing framework. **ISO/IEC 33020:2019** defines an ordinal six-level capability scale (0 Incomplete → 5 Innovating) and a four-point achievement rating (**N/P/L/F** — Not / Partially / Largely / Fully achieved, with defined percentage bands and P±/L± refinement); **ISO/IEC 33002:2015** sets the objectivity, repeatability and assessor-competence requirements a conformant assessment must meet; **ISO/IEC 33003:2015** governs the construction and validation of such measurement frameworks.
  - **But:** the family measures **process capability/maturity, not individual human competence** — its process-attribute model is process-shaped, and 33003's scope is process quality characteristics, not persons. Its value here is as a measurement-discipline precedent — defined tiers, an evidence-based achievement rating, and assessor-objectivity requirements — on which an individual-competence instrument could be modelled, with the caveat that person-level measurement additionally requires the validity and reliability evidence the process-assessment series does not supply. It reinforces §5's observation that the machinery for closing the gap already exists: person-certification machinery (ISO/IEC 17024/24773) and a levels-and-rating measurement discipline (ISO/IEC 330xx) both exist, and neither has been applied to AI-literacy competence.

**Net effect on the finding.** Each reference maps onto an existing category — a literacy model (DigComp), an organisational-maturity model (AIRI), HR-process and competence-management standards (ISO 30400/30422, ISO 10015), and a measurement-method precedent (ISO/IEC 330xx). None is an Article-4-native, deployer-workforce, comparable instrument that measures individual AI-literacy competence. The review therefore broadened the evidence base without changing the §4 pattern or the §5 gap.

## 5. The gap

**There is no openly-adoptable, Article-4-native standard for measuring AI literacy in the deployer workforce that yields comparable results across organisations, and no current CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 work item targets one.** Established three ways:

- The survey finds no framework scoring on all three primary criteria;
- The person-certification machinery (ISO/IEC 17024/24773) exists but carries no AI-literacy scheme;
- The JTC 21 work programme and the ten M/593 areas contain no literacy-measurement deliverable (§6).

Three observations underpin it:

- **The field measures organisations and systems; Article 4 is about persons.**
  - Maturity and governance models self-rate organisational process (or, for OWASP/DeepMind, the system/model).
  - The individual-assessment traditions that *do* measure persons (SFIA, EN 16234-1, OSCE/SJT, 17024-based schemes) are not AI-literacy-specific.
  - The person-level, AI-literacy intersection is empty.
- **Completion is a weak proxy for competence — with evidence.**
  - In practice, competence is often proxied by training completion or self-assessment; the training-transfer literature shows these correlate weakly with on-the-job transfer.
  - The direction is robust across the classic sources — Alliger et al. (1997) on the reaction/learning-versus-transfer gap; Baldwin & Ford (1988) on partial transfer; Blume et al. (2010), a meta-analysis of 89 studies finding transfer contingent on ability, conscientiousness, motivation and a supportive work environment rather than completion.
- **The machinery for closing the gap already exists.**
  - ISO/IEC 17024 and 24773 show the established route from general person-certification requirements to a domain-specific, portable competence scheme.
  - That an AI-literacy scheme has not been built on this route *is* the gap — and the reason it is tractable.

## 6. Location within the European standardisation programme

**Standardisation Request M/593** (Commission Implementing Decision C(2023)3215, accepted 2023; amended by **M/613**, under which the current AI-Act standards are prepared) requests standards across **ten areas** — risk management; datasets governance/quality; record-keeping; transparency; human oversight; accuracy; robustness; cybersecurity; quality management; conformity assessment.

- **AI literacy / staff competence is not among them.** Competence measurement falls in the interstice of the mandate — adjacent to human oversight (area 5), but not covered by it.

**Confirmed in the actual drafts.** The risk-management draft **prEN 18228:2026 (AI Risk Management)**, prepared by JTC 21 under M/613:

- Maps in its Annex ZA **only to Article 9 (risk management)** of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — not to Article 4, 14, or 26.
- Where it touches competence at all (§4.3, "Competence of personnel"), it requires the provider's *risk-management team* be competent but specifies **no method to measure, evidence, or compare** it — a competence *requirement* with no measurement instrument, mirroring ISO/IEC 42001 Clause 7.2.

**The pattern is consistent across the programme:** competence recurs as an unmeasured sub-requirement — never a measured object, and never for the deployer workforce that Article 4 addresses.

| M/593 area | JTC 21 work item (indicative) | Addresses staff *competence* measurement? |
|---|---|:--:|
| Risk management | prEN 18228 | ○ (Annex ZA maps to Art. 9 only; §4.3 competence unmeasured) |
| Datasets governance/quality | prEN 18284 | ○ |
| Record-keeping / transparency / human oversight | prEN 18229-1 | ○ (system oversight design, not overseer competence) |
| Accuracy / robustness | prEN 18229-2, prEN 18281 | ○ |
| Quality management system | prEN 18286 | ○ (org QMS; competence not measured) |
| Conformity assessment | prEN 18285 | ○ |
| **AI literacy (Art. 4)** | **— (no work item)** | **— (the gap)** |

**The seam with the human-oversight work.**

- **prEN 18229-1** specifies the *system* affordances that make oversight possible (logging, transparency, controls).
- The item proposed here would specify the *person* competence protocol — whether the assigned overseer can actually exercise that oversight.
- The interface between the two is **Article 14(4)** (capabilities the system must enable in the overseer) read with **Article 26(2)** (the deployer's duty to assign a competent overseer).
- A competence-measurement deliverable is therefore an **input to, not a competitor of**, the oversight work: 18229-1 makes the system oversee-able; a competence standard establishes that the human is competent to oversee it.

**Vehicle: a voluntary European Standard, not a harmonised one.** A *harmonised* standard under Article 40 confers a presumption of conformity with the **high-risk requirements** (Chapter III, §2) — a mechanism that does not extend to Article 4 (Chapter I). With Article 4 now a best-efforts duty, the right vehicle is a **voluntary European Standard (EN) or Technical Specification (TS)** — adopted to evidence diligence and referenceable by Member States, with the untouched **Article 26(2)** duty as its firmest anchor for the high-risk tier. A **CEN Workshop Agreement (CWA)** is a faster, lighter route to an initial specification.

**Why European-original work, not simply an ISO/IEC SC 42 item.** The Vienna Agreement defaults to ISO-lead, but preserves European-original standardisation where a standard must support EU legislation. A standard that gives content to a specific EU legal obligation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is European-original by nature — ISO/IEC deliverables are not written to that anchor. It should still **align with ISO/IEC 22989 terminology and reuse the ISO/IEC 17024/24773 certification model**: the AI-Act anchor makes it European-original, built on the ISO/IEC machinery rather than duplicating it.

**Why not simply a work item at CEN/TC 428?** A fair question — TC 428 may well be the right home: it owns the European competence machinery (WG 2), and any deliverable ignoring EN 16234-1 would create a competing competence vocabulary. Two things stand in the way of simply placing it there:

- TC 428's published AI position (white paper N 557, June 2023) draws ICT professionalism around those who *design, develop and maintain* AI, treating deployers/users as external stakeholders — and it predates the adopted Article 4. An Article-4 competence deliverable sits outside that self-set frame, and extending it is TC 428's decision to make.
- The legal anchor is a specific EU regulation — JTC 21's domain rather than TC 428's.

The recommendation is therefore **not** to pick a venue from outside, but to propose the item in **liaison between JTC 21** (which holds the AI Act anchor and the human-oversight work) **and CEN/TC 428** (which holds the competence vocabulary, proficiency scale and body of knowledge to reuse). Where it is finally housed is for those committees to decide; that it must not duplicate e-CF is not in doubt.

## 7. The shape of the missing standard

Independent of any particular methodology, the gap analysis implies the properties a solution should have. Each property below is traceable to a finding above, not to any product:

- **Competence-specific and deployer-workforce-scoped** *(from primary criterion ① and §5 observation 1)* — measuring the literacy of those who *use and oversee* AI systems, not only those who build them, and not the organisation as an aggregate.
- **Article-4- and Article-26(2)-native** *(from primary criterion ② and §6)* — mapped to the operative requirements of Article 4, and to the human-oversight competence duty of Article 26(2) read with Article 14(4) for high-risk contexts, so that a result speaks directly to the obligations. (The Article 4 referent is the amended text under Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.)
- **Individual and workflow-anchored, but proportionate** *(from §5 observation 1 and the OSCE/SJT precedent in §3.2)* — assessing persons in the context of real tasks, while respecting the "best-efforts" and proportionality character of the duty: a workable standard must be *tiered and risk-based*, applying deeper assessment to high-authority oversight roles and lighter-touch attestation or sampling to the long tail, rather than mandating full individual assessment of every member of staff (which could itself be disproportionate). *Illustratively and independently of any methodology:* a person assigned to oversee a high-risk credit-scoring model warrants a fuller, scenario-based assessment than a staff member who occasionally consults an AI drafting aid — the same standard must be able to make that distinction.
- **Comparable** *(from primary criterion ③)* — yielding results comparable across organisations under a common protocol, ideally realisable as a certification scheme in the ISO/IEC 17024/24773 tradition.
- **Terminologically aligned** *(from §3.3 and §6)* — to ISO/IEC 22989, and interoperable with the JTC 21 trustworthiness framework; and to the European competence vocabulary and proficiency scale of EN 16234-1 (e-CF), so that a result is expressible in the language European competence standardisation already uses rather than in a parallel one.
- **Openly adoptable** *(from secondary characteristic 5 and the SME-access point in §2)* — accessible to SMEs and public bodies, recognising that a voluntary (not mandated) standard depends on low-friction adoption.

Critically, such a standard should be positioned as **evidence of diligence, not a presumption of conformity.** Only a harmonised standard cited in the Official Journal under Article 40 confers a presumption of conformity, and that mechanism runs to the high-risk requirements of Chapter III — it does not extend to Article 4 at all. A competence result should therefore be understood as evidence that an organisation has taken measures to support AI literacy under Article 4 (as amended) and assigned competent oversight under Article 26(2), not as a compliance certificate.
